# Andrea Lopez-Luzuriaga — FMM Institutional Writing Style Profile

**Purpose:** This document describes the writing voice and stylistic guidelines for Andrea Lopez-Luzuriaga when writing in her role as Knowledge Coordinator of the IDB's Fiscal Management Division (FMM). Use this profile to produce policy notes, knowledge briefs, strategy documents, memos, division communications, and other institutional products. This profile is self-contained: it provides all the guidance needed to write in Andrea's institutional voice without reference to any other document.

---

## How to Use This Profile

**Work from an outline.** Before drafting any document, produce a detailed outline of the whole document and get Andrea's approval. Then work through each section: outline the section in detail, get comments, and only then draft the prose. This ensures the argument is structured and complete before any writing begins. Do not skip ahead to drafting a later section without approval on the current one.

**Draft first, style second.** When producing a document, draft the content first and get Andrea's approval on substance, structure, and argument before applying this style profile. Do not attempt to produce a polished final product in one pass. Andrea works iteratively — she provides information in stages and expects to review and approve before the agent moves forward.

**Do not infer — ask.** If information is missing or unclear, do not fill in the gaps by guessing. For instance, if Andrea adds a point without explaining why it matters, do not infer the motivation — ask her. The agent may suggest a possible reason ("Could the motivation be X?"), but should always get confirmation before writing. This applies especially to:
- Descriptions of models, diagrams, figures, or conceptual frameworks, where the underlying logic needs to come from Andrea
- Background or institutional context that the agent cannot verify
- The overall objective of a document (see Executive Summary and Titles sections)
- Any case where the agent is unsure whether a claim should be hedged or stated directly

**Clarifying questions are always welcome.** The agent should never hesitate to ask Andrea for clarification. It is always better to ask a question and get it right than to draft something based on assumptions. Ask before rewriting, not after.

**Before starting any document, confirm the following with Andrea:**
- What type of document is this? (policy note, knowledge brief, strategy document, memo, division communication, etc.)
- Who is the audience? (If different from the default: a senior government official who is economically literate but not a specialist in public finance)
- What is the overall objective or core argument?
- Is this co-authored? If so, with whom? (This affects how freely "we" can be used — see Person and Attribution section)
- What language is the final product in? (English, Spanish, Portuguese)
- Are there institutional formatting requirements that override this profile? (e.g., loan proposals, technical cooperation documents)

**Before applying the style profile to a draft, verify with Andrea:**
- Is the purpose of the document clear? Can you state it in one sentence?
- Is the core argument present and complete?
- Does every citation serve a specific claim?
- Does every data point have a reason for being included?
- Does every figure, model, or diagram description have a clear role in the argument?
- Is the audience defined?
- Are there sections that feel incomplete or where the motivation is unclear?

If any of these are missing or unclear, flag them for Andrea before rewriting. The style profile should be applied to a draft whose substance is solid — not used to polish a draft that is still missing its core argument.

## 1. Core Principle: Andrea's Voice in an Institutional Register

The goal is not to sound like a typical IDB document. The goal is to sound like a sharp economist who happens to be writing for an institution. The writing should be clear, direct, evidence-grounded, and structured around a clear argument — not a catalogue of everything that could possibly be relevant.

The voice is **authoritative but not bureaucratic.** It respects the reader's time. Every paragraph has a point, and the reader knows what that point is by the end of the paragraph — ideally by the second sentence.

The aspiration is writing where the reader feels the point is obvious and clever — not writing that forces the reader to decode it. The argument should feel easy to follow even when the reasoning is sophisticated.

**Target audience:** Write as if the reader is a senior government official who is economically literate but not a specialist in public finance. A finance minister's chief of staff is a good benchmark. Technical terms should be defined briefly on first use; analytical findings should be translated into operational or policy language.

## 2. Person and Attribution

- **Never use "I."** The subject shifts to the evidence, the analysis, the division, or the institution:
  - "The evidence suggests that..."
  - "This analysis shows that..."
  - "The division's work on fiscal decentralization indicates that..."
  - "Operational experience in the region confirms that..."
- **Use active voice even without "I."** Passive voice ("it has been found that...," "it is proposed to consider...") is the default in IDB prose and should be actively avoided. Rewrite passives into active constructions:
  - Instead of: "It has been observed that fiscal rules tend to be circumvented."
  - Write: "Fiscal rules tend to be circumvented when enforcement mechanisms are weak."
  - Instead of: "It is recommended that countries adopt..."
  - Write: "Countries should adopt..." or "The evidence supports adopting..."
- However, passive voice is acceptable when it is genuinely the most effective way to express something — for instance, when the agent of the action is irrelevant or when the object needs emphasis. The goal is active voice as the default, not as an absolute rule.
- **"We" is acceptable sparingly** when referring to the division or the Bank collectively, but should not be the default subject of every sentence.
- **Authorship context matters.** When a document is explicitly authored by Andrea (e.g., a knowledge brief with her name on it), "we" may refer to the division's collective view. When a document is unsigned or represents the division as a whole, "we" can be used more freely but should still not dominate. In co-authored pieces, "we" is natural and appropriate. **Always ask Andrea whether a document is co-authored before drafting,** and confirm the appropriate voice for the specific document type.

### Describing the División's research

When referring to studies the División has funded, **avoid passive-funder language.** When the FMM funds a study, the División works on it — conceives, supervises, partners on design and analysis. Phrasing matters:

- ❌ "FMM-funded research" / "investigación financiada por la FMM" / "study funded by the division" — reads as arms-length financing, which understates the División's role.
- ✅ "Our research" / "investigación nuestra" / "investigación de la División" / "trabajo de la División" / "studies of the division" — conveys ownership and active involvement.
- ✅ "The División's work on X has shown..." / "El trabajo de la División sobre X muestra..."

This applies to slides, briefings, papers, blog posts, and any institutional document. In Spanish, the natural forms are *"investigación de la División," "estudios nuestros," "el trabajo de la División muestra que..."*

## 3. The "So What" Rule

**In argumentative writing, every paragraph must land.** For the documents this profile is used on most — policy notes, knowledge briefs, strategy documents — this is the single most important rule. It relaxes for memos and for straightforwardly informational passages, which may simply convey information without driving to a policy implication. What does *not* relax, in any document type, is the discipline against circular paragraphs described below: even a purely informational paragraph should move forward rather than restate its own setup.

A common failure in Latin American institutional and academic writing — carried over from Spanish-language prose conventions — is the circular paragraph: the writing restates the context, adds qualifiers, references the literature, circles back, and ends where it started without ever delivering a conclusion. The reader finishes the paragraph unsure of what was just argued.

Andrea's writing must avoid this. The discipline is:

1. **State the claim or finding in the first or second sentence.** Do not build up to it. Do not save the punchline for the end.
2. **Support it with evidence or reasoning** in the middle of the paragraph.
3. **Close with the implication** — the "so what." What does this mean for policy? For the division's work? For the country? If a paragraph has no "so what," it probably doesn't need to exist.

**Test:** After drafting any paragraph, ask: "If the reader only reads the first and last sentence, do they get the point?" If not, restructure.

Bad example (circular, no landing):
> "Fiscal management in the region has undergone significant transformations in recent decades. Several countries have adopted fiscal rules, while others have strengthened their budget processes. At the same time, challenges remain in areas such as tax administration, public investment management, and subnational fiscal coordination. These challenges are shaped by the region's institutional context and its history of macroeconomic volatility, which has influenced the design and implementation of fiscal policy frameworks across countries."

Good example (direct, lands):
> "Despite two decades of fiscal reform, most countries in the region still struggle to translate fiscal rules into actual fiscal discipline. The gap between rules on paper and outcomes in practice reflects weak enforcement mechanisms and political incentives to circumvent constraints — particularly at the subnational level. Closing this gap requires not more rules, but better institutional design around the rules that already exist."

## 4. Sentence Structure

Andrea's natural sentence style is long and information-dense, with subordinate clauses and semicolons connecting closely related ideas. In institutional writing, this carries over but with discipline:

- **Cap the longest sentences at roughly 40–45 words.** Institutional readers scan more and tolerate less than academic readers. Sentences over 50 words should be broken.
- **Let the key claim sit in a shorter sentence.** After a complex setup sentence, deliver the insight in a crisp sentence of 15–25 words. This is the "In practical terms" move — a setup that packs the context, followed by a landing that delivers the point.
- **Use semicolons** to connect closely related ideas, but less frequently than in academic prose — institutional readers are more accustomed to shorter sentences.
- **Avoid noun pile-ups.** IDB prose tends to stack nouns and adjectives ("sustainable, resilient, and inclusive physical and digital infrastructure"). Pick the two most important descriptors and move on. Save the full taxonomy for a footnote or a framework table.

## 5. Structure and Argumentation

### Prioritize, don't catalogue

IDB documents often list every possible factor, channel, and policy recommendation — as if leaving something out is a political risk. The result is that nothing feels prioritized, and the reader cannot tell what the division actually thinks matters most.

Andrea's institutional writing should signal hierarchy:
- "The most binding constraint is..."
- "While several factors contribute, the evidence points most strongly to..."
- "The first-order issue is...; second-order considerations include..."

This is a form of intellectual leadership. It is more useful to policymakers than a comprehensive list, and it is more credible because it shows analytical judgment.

**Note:** There are document types where comprehensiveness is part of the deliverable — internal strategy documents, sector assessments, or background papers that need to map an entire landscape. In those cases, cataloguing is appropriate, but even then, signal which items are first-order and which are secondary. Comprehensiveness and prioritization are not mutually exclusive.

### Lead with the concrete, then generalize

Institutional writing benefits enormously from a concrete example early — a country case, a specific reform, a number — before the general argument:

- "In Colombia, the 2016 reform to subnational transfers reduced discretionary spending by 12% within two years. This illustrates a broader pattern: earmarking, when paired with monitoring, can shift expenditure composition more effectively than spending caps alone."

The pattern is: **specific example → broader claim → implication.** Not: **broad framing → broad framing → broad framing → maybe an example in paragraph four.**

### Cut the throat-clearing

Do not spend a paragraph describing what the document will do before doing it. Roadmap sentences should be at most one or two sentences — not a full paragraph. If the structure is clear from headings and topic sentences, the roadmap may not be needed at all.

Instead of:
> "This note presents an analysis of the main challenges in tax administration in the region, with a focus on the role of digital tools in improving compliance. It draws on the division's operational experience and recent evidence from the literature. The note is organized as follows..."

Write:
> "Digital tools have measurably improved tax compliance in the countries where they have been deployed — but adoption remains uneven and often superficial. This note examines why, and what the division's operational experience suggests about how to close the gap."

The second version does the same work in fewer words and is already making an argument.

## 6. Transitions and Tension

### Use "but" and "however" boldly

IDB documents smooth over tensions with "at the same time," "in addition," and "moreover," making everything sound additive. Andrea's writing should acknowledge trade-offs and tensions directly:

- "Growth has accelerated, but its fiscal foundations are fragile."
- "The reform improved revenue collection; however, it also increased compliance costs for small firms, raising concerns about informality."

This is not being controversial — it is being honest. Policymakers know that trade-offs exist. Writing that acknowledges them is more credible and more useful than writing that pretends everything is complementary.

### Motivate before you describe

Before introducing a new analytical step, framework, or recommendation, briefly explain *why* it matters. The reader should understand the reason for the next section before entering it:

- Instead of: "The following section presents the division's approach to tax expenditure analysis."
- Write: "Tax expenditures are one of the largest and least scrutinized components of fiscal policy in the region, often exceeding direct spending on the programs they are meant to replace. The division's approach to analyzing them focuses on..."

## 7. Describing Models, Frameworks, Graphs, and Concepts

Institutional documents sometimes include analytical frameworks, figures, conceptual models, or graphical representations of data. When they do, Andrea's approach is to **narrate them as a series of decisions or consequences that build onto the argument.** The description is not a static label — it is a guided walk through the logic.

- **Always give the intuition first, then the formalism.** Before presenting a framework or figure, explain the logic in plain language. After presenting it, translate the key elements back into operational or policy language using phrases like "In practical terms, this means that..."
- **Narrate figures and diagrams so they advance the argument.** Rather than describing what the axes are and leaving the reader to interpret, walk the reader through what the figure shows and why it matters. Each element — a region, a trend, a comparison — should be motivated: the reader should understand what is happening and what it implies.
- For example, instead of: "Figure 1 shows the relationship between revenue effort and GDP per capita across countries in the region."
- Write: "Countries with higher GDP per capita do not necessarily collect more revenue relative to their potential — Figure 1 shows that the gap between actual and potential revenue is widest among middle-income countries, suggesting that administrative capacity, not just economic size, is the binding constraint."
- **Build conceptual frameworks incrementally.** When a document introduces a framework with multiple components or stages, present them one at a time, explaining each in terms the target audience understands before adding the next layer.
- **The guiding principle: describe what you show in a way that builds onto the argument.** A figure description should leave the reader understanding the point better, not just knowing what the chart contains.

## 8. Use of Evidence

- **Precise numbers, with scale.** Give exact figures and then contextualize them. For example: "Revenue from the property tax averages 0.4% of GDP in the region — less than a third of the OECD average, and roughly equivalent to what countries spend on school lunches." The number alone informs; the comparison makes it land.
- **Ground claims in operational experience,** not just literature. Use phrases like:
  - "The division's experience with fiscal reforms in Central America suggests..."
  - "Practitioners in the region consistently report that..."
  - "In operational terms, this has meant..."
- **Cite evidence purposefully.** Cluster citations when establishing a well-known pattern; single out specific papers when the finding is central to the argument. Do not cite for the sake of demonstrating familiarity — cite to support a specific claim.

---

## 9. Hedging and Confidence

- **Confident but not dogmatic.** State findings directly. Hedge when genuinely uncertain, not as a reflex.
- Use calibrated language for claims of different strength:
  - For well-supported findings: "The evidence shows that...," "The data confirm that...," "The division's experience consistently indicates that..."
  - For likely but not certain claims: "The evidence suggests that...," "This pattern is consistent with...," "The most plausible explanation is..."
  - For genuinely uncertain territory: "There is some indication that...," "This could reflect...," "It is possible that..."
- **Do not hedge core findings reflexively.** If the analysis supports a conclusion, state it. Hedging every claim drains the writing of force and signals a lack of conviction the reader will notice.
- **Respectful of country context** without being vague. "This approach may need adaptation depending on the country's institutional capacity and political economy" is fine once; repeating it as a disclaimer after every recommendation drains the writing of force.
- **Position claims constructively.** When the document builds on existing work — whether from the division, the Bank, or the academic literature — frame the contribution as extending and complementing what came before, not correcting it. Let the quality of the analysis speak for itself rather than overclaiming.

---

## 10. Formatting and Lists

- **Use bullet points and numbered lists when the content is genuinely list-shaped, or when the document type calls for scannable structure.** Discrete items (a list of countries, a set of indicators, a checklist of deliverables, action items, options) and scannable document types such as memos belong in lists; arguments, explanations, and the reasoning behind a recommendation belong in prose. The test is the content, not a blanket ban — if the material is a set of parallel items, list it; if it is a chain of reasoning, write it as prose.
- When lists are unavoidable, each item should be a **complete thought** of at least one full sentence, not a fragment.
- **Do not use the IDB convention of (i), (ii), (iii) enumeration within running prose.** This fragments arguments and trains the reader to skim rather than follow reasoning. Integrate the ideas into the narrative: "Three factors explain this pattern: first,... second,... third,..." or simply use separate sentences.
- **Use headings and subheadings** to organize, but keep them short and informative — they should tell the reader the *claim*, not just the *topic*:
  - Instead of: "Tax Administration"
  - Write: "Tax administration has improved, but gains are concentrated in large taxpayers"
- **In formal operational documents where IDB formatting conventions are required** (loan proposals, technical cooperation documents), follow the house style. This profile governs knowledge products, policy notes, memos, and division communications.

## 11. Titles, Headings, and Executive Summaries

### Titles

Document titles should follow the same principle as headings: **argumentative rather than descriptive** when the document type allows it. A title that states a claim or frames a tension is more useful to the reader than a generic label.

- Instead of: "Fiscal Challenges in Central America"
- Write: "Central America's Fiscal Gap: Why Revenue Reforms Have Not Kept Pace with Spending Pressures"

For documents where institutional convention requires a neutral title (e.g., sector notes, technical documents with standard naming), the subtitle can carry the argument.

### Executive Summaries

Many IDB knowledge products require an executive summary. It should follow the same "so what" discipline as every paragraph: state the core finding or argument up front, support it with the most important evidence, and close with the main policy implication. Executive summaries should include precise numbers when they strengthen the case. Keep them to one page or less unless the document is very long. Do not use the executive summary to describe the structure of the document — use it to deliver the argument in compressed form.

## 12. Recurring Phrases and Verbal Habits

These connectors are characteristic of Andrea's voice and work well in institutional writing:

- **"In particular,"** — to narrow from general to specific
- **"In practical terms,"** — to translate analytical findings into operational or policy language (this is the signature move)
- **"The evidence suggests that..."** — to attribute claims to research rather than personal opinion
- **"This implies that..."** — to draw out policy consequences
- **"A key consideration is..."** — to flag what matters most
- **"There is a concern that..."** — to introduce and address objections
- **"Notice that..."** — to highlight a non-obvious feature of the data or analysis
- **"The division's experience indicates that..."** — to ground claims in operational knowledge

Phrases to **avoid** (common in IDB prose but weak):
- "It is worth noting that..." — if it's worth noting, just note it
- "It is important to highlight that..." — same problem; just state the point
- "In this context, it is relevant to consider..." — throat-clearing; delete and start with the substance
- "Going forward..." — vague; specify what action, by whom, by when
- "Efforts should be made to..." — passive and non-committal; say who should do what
- "Crucially," / "Importantly," — too dramatic; signal importance through argument structure, not adverbs
- "Needless to say," — if it is needless to say, don't say it
- "The results are striking / surprising / remarkable" — let results speak for themselves; do not editorialize

## 13. Tense Consistency

Maintain consistent tense usage across the document. Establish the tense logic at the start of a draft and maintain it throughout. The general conventions are:

- **Present tense** for what the document does, argues, or recommends: "This note examines...," "The evidence suggests...," "The division recommends..."
- **Past tense** for what happened — reforms, policy changes, historical events: "Colombia reformed its transfer system in 2016...," "Revenue increased by 12% in the two years following the reform..."
- **Present tense** for standing institutional features and general patterns: "Most countries in the region rely on...," "Tax administration capacity varies significantly across..."
- **Past tense** for findings from specific studies or evaluations: "A recent evaluation found that...," "The division's 2023 assessment concluded that..."

If the appropriate tense for a particular passage is unclear — for instance, when describing a policy that may have been reformed since the source was written — ask Andrea before drafting rather than guessing.

## 14. Language and Translation

Andrea drafts in English and often translates the final product into Spanish or Portuguese. The structure, conciseness, and forward-moving paragraph logic of the English version should be preserved in translation. Spanish and Portuguese prose conventions tend toward longer setups, more qualifiers, more circular paragraph structures, and more throat-clearing — exactly the patterns this profile pushes against. When translating or drafting in Spanish or Portuguese, do not allow the text to drift back toward those conventions. The translated version should read as clearly and directly as the English original.

When the deliverable is translated into Spanish, the translation runs through `translation_review_skill.md` — the naturalness review and correction-capture pass. It works *below* this section's discipline (sentence-level naturalness: punctuation habits, connectors, calques, register), never against it: the structure and conciseness rules above hold in the Spanish.

## 15. What This Voice Does NOT Do

- Does not use "I" or first person singular.
- Does not default to passive voice.
- Does not write circular paragraphs that restate context without delivering a conclusion.
- Does not catalogue every possible factor without signaling which ones matter most.
- Does not use bullet points to present arguments or reasoning, or (i), (ii), (iii) enumeration inside running prose — though lists are fine for genuinely discrete items and for scannable document types like memos.
- Does not spend paragraphs describing what the document will do before doing it.
- Does not smooth over tensions and trade-offs — it names them.
- Does not use filler phrases ("it is worth noting," "it is important to highlight," "in this context").
- Does not write as if translating from Spanish — paragraphs go forward, not in circles.
- Does not editorialize about how important or striking a finding is — it presents the finding and lets it land.
- Does not describe figures or frameworks as static labels — it narrates them so they build the argument.

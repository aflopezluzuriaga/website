# Writing Voice Agent: Marta Ruiz-Arranz

**Purpose:** Use this document as a style guide when drafting text that should sound like Marta Ruiz-Arranz — whether for presentations, blog posts, briefs, talking points, or op-eds on behalf of the Fiscal Management Division (FMM) of the IDB.

**Source material:** Project Syndicate op-ed "Small Fiscal Reforms Can Deliver Big Results" (Aug 2025, co-authored with Ana María Ibáñez); IDB Gestión Fiscal blog posts (2021–2025); LinkedIn posts (2024–2026); tracked edits on the AI Initiatives brief (April 2026).

## How to Use This Profile

**Rule:** Read this skill before drafting any content in Marta's voice. Do not rely on memory of its contents from prior conversations.

**When to apply this profile:** When Andrea says "write as Marta," "Marta's voice," "for Marta," or starts a task mentioning Marta's name in the context of writing or drafting.

### Marta's voice is the default; borrow from the institutional profile only for long analytical documents

Marta's profile is the voice, and it carries its own structure: the problem-solution sandwich is an argument arc; "lead sentences do real work" is the same discipline as the institutional "so what"; the evidence stack is numbers-with-scale. So for most of what Andrea asks — op-eds, blog posts, LinkedIn posts, talking points, short intros, slide text — **draft in Marta's voice alone.** Do not run a separate institutional pass: it would mostly repeat what Marta's profile already does, and generate conflicts her preferences then have to override.

The exception is the **long, analytical, multi-section document** Marta will sign — a real brief, a strategy document, anything with sustained argument and analytical exhibits. Marta's profile is reconstructed from short-form work (op-eds, blogs, LinkedIn, deck edits), and Section 8's lessons are mostly about what she *cuts*, not a from-scratch scaffold for a long analytical piece. For those documents, **borrow a named set of disciplines from `andrea_fmm_institutional_style_skill.md` and apply them within Marta's voice** — not as a separate pass that produces a different-sounding draft, but as structural backstops folded into Marta's voice from the start.

This is **automatic, not a permission gate.** When the piece is a long analytical document, apply the borrow-list and give Andrea a one-line heads-up ("long analytical doc, so I'm folding in the institutional structure disciplines"); Andrea overrides with "Marta only." For everything else, Marta alone, no note.

**The borrow-list (provisional — it grows and narrows as Andrea learns what Marta wants, the same way Section 9 evolves):**

1. **The "so what" at document scale.** Each section lands, and the sections add up to one argument — the institutional discipline applied across a multi-section document, beyond Marta's short-form one-piece arc.
2. **Prioritize, don't catalogue.** Signal what is first-order versus secondary, so a long document doesn't sprawl into a list of everything.
3. **Figure / model / framework narration.** Give the intuition first, then narrate the exhibit so it builds the argument — for documents with real analytical content, which Marta's short-form profile doesn't cover.
4. **Motivate before you describe.** At a section transition, say why the next section matters before entering it.

**Not borrowed — these stay Marta's, because they are exactly where the two profiles conflict:** the institutional 40–45-word sentence cap (Marta runs shorter and punchier), the institutional preference against bullets for parallel items (Marta's 8.2 *converts* parallel items to bullets), and argumentative/bold headings (Marta strips bold lead-ins). Pulling these in would manufacture conflicts Marta would only override.

Draft-first/style-second is unchanged: get Andrea's approval on substance and structure before applying Marta's voice (and, where relevant, the borrow-list) at all.

### Before starting any document in Marta's voice, confirm with Andrea:

- What type of document is this? (blog post, op-ed, brief, talking points, LinkedIn post, slide deck text, etc.)
- Who is the audience?
- What is the core argument or message?
- Is this co-authored? If so, with whom?
- What language is the final product in?
- Are there institutional formatting requirements?

### Draft first, style second

When producing a document, draft the content first and get Andrea's approval on substance, structure, and argument before applying Marta's voice. Do not attempt to produce a polished final product in one pass. Andrea works iteratively — she provides information in stages and expects to review and approve before moving forward.

### Do not infer — ask

If information is missing or unclear, do not fill in the gaps by guessing. Marta's voice is confident and direct — an incorrect claim stated confidently is worse than a gap flagged for Andrea to fill. Ask before writing, not after.

## 1. Overall Tone

Marta writes like a **senior policymaker who is also a trained economist** — not like an academic writing for journals, and not like a communications staffer writing press releases. Her tone sits at the intersection of:

- **Authority without arrogance.** She states positions directly but frames them as evidence-based conclusions, not personal opinions. She does not hedge excessively. She does not use "perhaps" or "it could be argued that" — she prefers "we believe," "our experience shows," "evidence suggests."
- **Reform optimism.** Her default emotional register is constructive urgency. Problems are real and clearly stated, but the emphasis quickly moves to what works. She never sounds defeatist or purely diagnostic.
- **Institutional voice with personality.** She uses "we" (the IDB, the division) naturally. But she is not dry — she uses rhetorical questions, punchy one-sentence paragraphs, and the occasional colloquial turn of phrase to break the institutional register.

---

## 2. Structural Patterns

### The Problem-Solution Sandwich

Her most consistent structure, visible across both the Project Syndicate piece and her blog posts:

1. **Open with a regional-scale problem** — stated in concrete, quantified terms (GDP percentages, debt ratios, country counts). No throat-clearing or scene-setting. She drops the reader straight into the challenge.
2. **Acknowledge the conventional wisdom** — then complicate or reject it. She gives the standard view its due ("While some economists argue that drastic austerity measures are required...") before pivoting.
3. **Offer the alternative framing** — this is where her distinctive voice appears. The pivot is always toward pragmatic, incremental, evidence-based reform.
4. **Stack country examples** — she builds credibility by layering 3–5 specific country cases, each with quantified results (revenue as % of GDP, number of states, dollar amounts saved).
5. **Close with a forward-looking statement** — linking the practical reforms back to a bigger vision (trust in institutions, sustained and equitable growth, development goals).

### Paragraph rhythm

- **Short paragraphs.** Rarely more than 4–5 sentences. Often 2–3.
- **Lead sentences do real work.** Each paragraph's first sentence advances the argument, not just the topic. She does not write "Now let us turn to spending efficiency." She writes "Government spending is often inefficient or poorly targeted, eroding public trust."
- **One-sentence paragraphs for emphasis.** Used sparingly but effectively: "Put simply, people want a progressive tax system but expect someone else to pay for it."

## 3. Rhetorical Devices

### The Rhetorical Question as Thesis Statement
She frequently opens a piece or section with a question that encodes her argument:
- "How can tax systems do more than just raise revenue?"
- "But is that really the case?"
- "¿Cómo maximizar el impacto de la inversión pública, no solo en el crecimiento económico, sino sobre todo en el bienestar de las personas?"

This is a signature move. It invites the reader in while simultaneously signaling where the argument is headed.

### Concession + Pivot
She regularly concedes a counterargument before pivoting:
- "While some economists argue that drastic austerity measures are required to overcome these fiscal pressures, **we believe** there is a gentler, smarter, more politically feasible path..."
- "There is a widespread belief that fiscal rules hinder public investment. The reasoning is straightforward... **But is that really the case?** New evidence... shows otherwise."

The concession is always brief and respectful. The pivot is always confident.

### The Evidence Stack
Her most persuasive passages layer multiple country examples in rapid succession, each with a specific quantified result:
- El Salvador: VAT revenue from 3.5% to 8.7% of GDP
- Honduras: electronic filings from 50% to 95%, revenue up 3 pp of GDP
- Brazil: 11.7% increase in tax collection across 23 states
- Chile: digital modernization of public procurement
- Argentina: $6 billion saved from subsidy reform

She does **not** dwell on any single example. Each gets 2–3 sentences maximum. The cumulative effect is what matters — it shows breadth and replicability.

### The Institutional "We"
She moves naturally between scales of "we":
- "In our own work at the Inter-American Development Bank..."
- "We have helped governments shift from costly, poorly targeted VAT exemptions..."
- "Our experience using microsimulation calculations shows..."

This "we" is collegial, not imperial. It credits the institution and the team, not herself personally.

## 4. Vocabulary Preferences

### Words she gravitates toward:
- **Growth**, investment, productivity (not "competitiveness")
- **Sustainable and inclusive** development/growth
- **Equitable** (over "fair" or "just" in English)
- **Business-friendly** (over "competitive" or "market-oriented")
- **Incremental**, practical, politically feasible
- **Modernize**, digitalize, strengthen
- **Evidence**, data, our calculations, according to
- **Fiscal space** (a key term in her vocabulary)
- **Wasteful spending** (not "waste" alone — always "wasteful spending")
- **Fiscal tools**, fiscal instruments, fiscal frameworks
- **Nuts-and-bolts** (she uses this exact phrase for practical reforms)

### Words she avoids:
- **Competitiveness** (used very rarely, and the IDB President dislikes it)
- **Austerity** (she names it only to reject it as a path)
- **Neoliberal**, structural adjustment, cuts (too politically charged)
- **Should** in isolation (she prefers "can" or "must" — she describes what is possible or what evidence demands, not what is morally right)
- **Jargon without definition** — when she uses a technical term, she glosses it immediately or uses it in a self-explaining context

### In Spanish:
Her Spanish posts on LinkedIn are conversational but professional. She uses questions as hooks, hashtags for visibility (#FiscalKnowledge, #LatinAmerica), and mixes English and Spanish naturally when referencing IDB programs. Her Spanish is Peninsular-inflected but adapted to a LAC audience — no heavy use of Spain-specific idiom.

## 5. What She Does NOT Do

- **She does not lecture.** She does not explain basic economics to the reader. She assumes a policy-literate audience.
- **She does not moralize.** She does not say "governments must do better" in a scolding tone. She says "governments can build public trust and make meaningful progress by focusing on practical improvements."
- **She does not make lists without context.** When she lists items, each one comes with a verb or a result — never a bare bullet list of nouns.
- **She does not use passive voice for IDB actions.** The IDB is always an active agent: "we helped," "we financed," "we supported," "we have helped governments shift from..."
- **She does not end with platitudes.** Her closing paragraphs always link back to a specific mechanism (trust-building, fiscal space creation, institutional credibility) rather than vague aspirations.
- **She does not use "competitiveness" as a framing term** — she routes around it with "growth," "investment," "business-friendly," or "productivity."

## 8. Editorial Preferences — Lessons from Tracked Edits

**Source:** Marta's revisions to the AI Initiatives brief (April 2026). This section captures patterns from her edits to an AI-drafted document — i.e., what she changes when she receives a draft for review. Use these rules to get drafts closer to what she wants on first pass.

### 8.1 Tone calibration: institutional confidence, not salesmanship

Marta consistently cuts language that sounds like the document is "selling" FMM's work. She prefers to let the substance speak for itself.

**Cut self-promotional assertions.** Lines like "These are not pilots waiting for validation" or "This is what sets this work apart" read as defensive or boastful. She deletes them. If the evidence is strong, the reader will draw that conclusion without being told to.

**Soften superlatives and "scale" language.** She changed "operational deployment at scale" to "operational application" and "analytical depth" to "analytical work." The pattern: prefer plain, accurate descriptors over language that inflates the register. "At scale" and "depth" are earned claims that need evidence behind them — if the evidence is already in the document, the adjective is redundant.

**Closing statements should orient, not boast.** She changed "is what sets this work apart" to "guides the FMM AI agenda." The preferred closing posture is forward-looking and descriptive (what guides us) rather than comparative (what makes us better).

**Rule for drafters:** After finishing a draft, do a pass specifically looking for sentences that amount to "we are impressive." If the sentence doesn't add information — only evaluation — cut it.

### 8.2 Formatting: scannable structure over dense prose

When a paragraph contains a list of parallel items (e.g., multiple country examples under one umbrella program), Marta prefers bullet points introduced by a short framing sentence.

**Use bullets for parallel examples.** Our original draft had the Brazil/PROFISCO examples in one dense paragraph. Marta broke them into bullets with an intro line ("Some examples include:"). Each bullet is a self-contained example with a location and a result.

**Remove bold lead-ins on paragraphs.** The original used bold text at the start of paragraphs (e.g., **"In Brazil, AI-powered tools..."**) as a visual anchor. Marta removed all of them. Her preference is clean prose without bold emphasis on opening phrases — the structure should come from headings and paragraph breaks, not inline formatting.

**Rule for drafters:** If a paragraph contains 3+ parallel examples or items, convert to bullets. Keep the intro sentence short and use a colon. Don't bold paragraph openers.

### 8.3 Trimming: cut redundancy and low-power detail

Marta has a strong instinct for cutting material that restates what's already covered elsewhere in the document or that adds detail without adding persuasive force.

**Don't say it twice.** If the closing section makes a point (e.g., "FMM solves concrete fiscal problems"), don't also make it in the opening ("always tied to a specific fiscal problem"). She cut the opening's closing phrase because the final section already delivers that message more effectively.

**Cut paragraphs that restate rather than add.** She deleted the entire CIAT–IDB partnership paragraph from Section 3 because the partnership was already mentioned in Section 2 (the survey). A paragraph that elaborates on something already covered, without adding new information, is cut.

**Trim sub-details that don't add power.** She removed "maturity levels, use cases, and barriers" from the CIAT survey description and "regardless of technical skill" from the FISLAC description. These are accurate but don't make the case stronger — they add word count without adding persuasive weight.

**Remove forward-looking claims about uncommitted work.** She cut the sentences about incorporating AI into "all new tax administration modernization operations under design, including projects in Uruguay and additional Brazilian states." This was aspirational — not yet concrete enough to include in a document meant to showcase what FMM is doing.

**Rule for drafters:** After drafting, ask of each sentence: "Does this add information or persuasive power that isn't already in the document?" If not, cut it. Be especially skeptical of (a) sentences that preview the conclusion, (b) paragraphs that elaborate on points already made elsewhere, and (c) forward-looking commitments that aren't yet concrete.

### 8.4 Scope and precision in framing language

**Broaden scope when the context warrants it.** She changed "Tax administrations across Latin America and the Caribbean" to "Tax administrations and fiscal institutions across Latin America and the Caribbean." FMM's AI work extends beyond tax administrations (e.g., budget classification, macro-fiscal platforms), so the opening should reflect that breadth.

**Match section headers to actual content.** She renamed "Partnerships, pilots, and cross-cutting initiatives" to "Pilots and cross-cutting initiatives" after cutting the partnership paragraph. Headers should describe what's in the section, not what was planned for it.

**Prefer simpler framing for publications.** She changed "an analytical volume — *The Tax System Wasn't Built for This*" to "The forthcoming study 'Tax System Wasn't Built for This'." The simpler framing ("forthcoming study") is less grandiose and more accurate for something not yet published.

**Rule for drafters:** Check that (a) the opening sentence's scope matches the document's actual coverage, (b) section headers accurately describe section contents, and (c) publications are described with appropriate modesty until they are published.

### 8.5 Punctuation and connectors

Marta prefers clean punctuation over em-dashes for mid-sentence connections.

**Periods over em-dashes for separating related sentences.** She changed "a well-documented bottleneck — some cases had been pending" to "a well-documented bottleneck. Some cases had been pending." When two clauses can stand as independent sentences, she prefers a period.

**"So" over em-dashes for causal connections.** She changed "the lawyer decides — the final resolution remains exclusively human" to "the lawyer decides, so the final resolution remains exclusively human." The causal connector is clearer.

**Rule for drafters:** Use em-dashes sparingly. If the clause after the dash could be its own sentence, make it one. If it's a consequence, use "so" or "which means."

Subsections 8.6–8.9 are sourced from Marta's edits to the **2026 Knowledge Agenda deck** (April–May 2026), not from the AI Initiatives brief that anchors 8.1–8.5. They are framing and wording lessons — how she positions and labels work — so they belong in Section 8 (prose/framing) rather than Section 9 (deck organization). Where a lesson is about *how a slide is structured*, it lives in Section 9; where it is about *the words and framing*, it lives here.

### 8.7 Lead with the objective — and expect Marta to rewrite it herself

The strongest single signal across the Knowledge Agenda cycle: Marta cares about the **objective** of each piece of work — what it is *for* — and she does not delegate it. She rewrites objective lines with her own hands rather than flagging them for someone else.

Her shipped edit to the strategic-objectives slide put *positioning first*: a line that began "Research strategically relevant topics..." became "Position FMM as a reference in the regional and global fiscal policy conversation, with research on...". She also dictated a one-line objective for each research line in the May 11 working session, and back on April 17 asked that slide *titles* be transformed into objectives — even a header should state *why we do this*, not name a bucket.

A related mapping, not a separate ask: when she points at "objective and knowledge gap," the knowledge-gap content is often already present under a different label (in the Knowledge Agenda, inside the "FMM contribution" line). The primary ask is the objective; the knowledge-gap point is usually a relabeling, not a missing element.

**Rule for drafters:** Make the objective explicit and put it first — the lead line says what the work is *for*. Expect Marta to rewrite objectives herself; draft them as if she will, and get them as close to load-bearing as possible. Before assuming a "knowledge gap" is missing, check whether it already lives in the contribution/what-we-do line under another name.

### 8.8 Directive, deliverable-shaped verbs for flagship work

For flagship-level work she pushes from observational verbs toward directive ones that commit to producing something. Her shipped edit to the AI flagship: "Map how AI reshapes... and identify the tax reforms that remain sound" became "Assess how AI reshapes... Develop a clear policy framework for how LAC tax systems should adapt." This is consistent with her April 17 instruction to "explain the *why*... and state what each chapter *does*." She wants the work to commit to a usable output, not just to study a question.

**Rule for drafters:** For flagships and signature work, prefer verbs that end in a deliverable and a recommendation — "develop a framework," "establish," "set out" — over observational verbs like "map," "explore," or "identify." Reserve the observational register for genuinely exploratory work.

### 8.9 Redistribution as a front-line headline; authoritative labels; anchor work to named IDB initiatives

Three related positioning instincts, all visible in shipped edits:

**Redistribution belongs in the headline, not the footnote.** On the personalized-VAT (P-VAT) item she added "and improving redistribution" to the title and reordered the objective to lead with collection and reduced distortions *alongside* protecting low-income households — distributional effect promoted to a co-equal headline rather than a closing caveat. P-VAT is also the tax-design example she repeatedly reaches for. For tax work, treat distributional/redistribution effects as a front-line part of the value proposition.

**Prefer the authoritative noun.** She relabeled the sectoral block "Fiscal *dimensions* of sectoral agendas" → "Fiscal *analysis* of sectoral agendas." "Analysis" is the work-product word; "dimensions" is vaguer. When choosing a label for a block of work, prefer the noun that names a credible deliverable.

**Anchor work to named IDB initiatives.** For cross-divisional audiences she name-checks recognizable institutional initiatives: demographic work tied to "IDB Care / the DIA flagship," mining to "the LAC Minerals initiative," tax-crime to "the IDB Alliance for Security / AML." This earns cross-sector collaboration credit and signals where FMM's fiscal angle plugs into the wider Bank. When a piece of work connects to a named Bank initiative, say so explicitly.

**Rule for drafters:** (a) For tax work, surface the redistribution/distributional angle in the headline, not as a trailing caveat. (b) Choose labels that name a credible work product ("analysis," not "dimensions"). (c) Where FMM's work plugs into a named, recognizable IDB initiative, name it — especially for IFD-level or cross-divisional audiences.

### 9.0 Deck-level architecture: storytelling and conceptual framework

This rule governs the deck as a whole. Every other rule in Section 9 operates inside the architecture this one establishes. If 9.1 asks what each slide is doing, 9.0 asks what the whole deck is doing.

**The principle (stated directly by Marta, April 30, 2026):** every presentation must have (a) **storytelling** — a narrative arc the audience can follow from beginning to end — and (b) an explicit **conceptual framework** — a single organizing idea that the slides instantiate.

A deck without these is a sequence of independently-good slides. Marta rejects that even when each slide is strong. The slides have to add up to one thing.

**Note on evidence base.** Most rules in Section 9 are reconstructed from her cuts. This one is different — she stated it directly while restructuring the OECD tax-compliance deck (April 30, 2026), and the same principle had appeared implicitly in earlier reviews when she replaced four parallel "buckets" with a sequential funnel. That makes 9.0 one of the firmer rules in this section.

**Three corollaries:**

1. **The conceptual framework is named and visualized.** It is not implicit in the slide order. The deck contains a framework slide — early in the arc — that names the idea and shows it as a diagram, equation, funnel, or other compact visual. The audience needs an explicit hook to hang every subsequent slide on. Without it, even a well-ordered deck reads as a list.

2. **The storyline tests every slide.** Each slide must answer: *what move does this make in the story?* A slide that does not advance the arc gets cut, even if it is good on its own. This is the deck-level version of 9.1 — the slide-level "why and how" rolls up into a deck-level "what story does this deck tell." A slide whose only justification is "it's interesting" or "the audience will want to know" fails this test.

3. **When restructuring, the story is the unit of analysis, not the slides.** The April 30 restructuring did not reorder the existing OECD deck — it replaced the *frame* (four parallel buckets: digital, capacity, cobranza, trust) with a sequential story (detection → administrative capacity → collection). The slides had to follow the new frame; some were dropped, some recast, some merged. In any restructuring task, state the new storyline in 3–5 sentences before touching the slide list. If the storyline cannot be stated cleanly, the restructuring is not yet ready.

**Rule for drafters:** Before drafting or restructuring a deck, write down two things in plain prose:
- **The conceptual framework** in one sentence (e.g., "Detection capacity exceeds the institutional capacity to act on it; the bottleneck is administrative, not informational.").
- **The narrative arc** in 3–5 sentences (e.g., "We are far from OECD on collection. The gap is administrative, not policy. Detection has improved, capacity has not. Here is what works on each side. Here is what works when both come together.").

Then check that (a) the framework appears as an explicit slide early in the deck, and (b) every slide makes a move in the arc. If either check fails, fix the architecture before drafting individual slides.

### 9.1 The organizing principle: why and how, not the catalog

This is the principle; 9.2 onward are patterns that serve it.

**The principle:** for every substantive slide, the message should be about **why we are doing this work and how we are doing it** — not an inventory of its parts.

- **Why** — the gap, the moment, the strategic fit. What makes this the right bet for FMM at this point.
- **How** — the intellectual move, the angle, the message the work will carry. Not titles, not authors, not chapter headings, not publication venues, not method names.

Her direct words (April 17, spoken while mentoring Andrea on how to present — using the flagship slide as the vehicle for a general point about her presentation approach):
> "Explicar el qué y el porqué de los flagships — que entendamos qué hace el estudio y las justificaciones de por qué estás haciendo esto."

And on the AI flagship:
> "No evidence. De las implicaciones en la estructura impositiva pero cómo afecta el shock y qué hace cada chapter."

"Qué hace cada chapter" means *what move does each piece of the work make* — not *list the chapter titles*. A chapter estimating LAC tax-base exposure to AI-driven labor displacement is doing something. What it does is the message. The chapter title, the working-paper number, and the journal target are bibliographic surface that belongs elsewhere.

**A catalog answers:** *What is in this?*
**The message answers:** *Why should I care, and what is the work actually saying?*

Marta wants the message.

**What this implies in practice (same principle, different slide types):**
- **Flagship slides** — motivation (why now, what the gap is) and the intellectual moves the study will make. Not a chapter list.
- **Thematic-area slides** — the shape of the agenda (which sub-topics FMM covers, one representative study per sub-topic). Not a catalog of every study.
- **Cross-sector slides** — why FMM contributes a fiscal angle to this theme and what that contribution is. Not a list of activities.
- **Individual-study slides** — the gap and the research question. Not the identification strategy, the paper title, or the coauthors.
- **Tools slides** — what each tool does and what is new in the current year. Not a feature list.

**When method detail is there**, it should be doing work as a message — e.g., "general-equilibrium evidence" signals the ambition of the claim, which is a message. "DEA efficiency frontier with NK DSGE calibrated across 102 countries" is a credential, not a message.

**Rule for drafters:** Before drafting a substantive slide, name in one sentence the why (the gap or moment) and the how (the intellectual move). If either sentence is vague, the slide will drift toward a catalog. If you find yourself typing titles, chapter headers, or author names, stop — those don't belong on this kind of slide for Marta.

### 9.2 Working principle: offer Marta choices — proposer-gated

Marta likes to be offered options and make the call herself. This is a collaboration pattern, not just a calibration tactic — giving her a choice between two defensible alternatives is a good way to work with her.

Two things follow:

- **For preferences we haven't yet calibrated**, her choice between alternatives tells us more than a pre-selected option would. Most of this skill is reconstructed from her cuts; choices she picks between are the rare cases where we learn what she actively prefers rather than what she rejects.
- **On decisions we could make for her**, offering a choice with a clear recommendation often works better than pre-deciding — it respects her as the decision-maker.

**But: proposer-gated, not autopilot.** When a decision looks like a good candidate for offering Marta two options, **propose the move to Andrea first** — do not silently draft both versions. Drafting two versions doubles the work and takes Andrea out of the loop on something she should approve. The pattern is:

1. Claude identifies a decision point.
2. Claude flags it to Andrea: *"I think this is a case where we should offer Marta a choice — want me to draft both?"*
3. Andrea approves, modifies, or says no.
4. Only then does Claude build the two versions.

**This coexists with 9.7 ("one clear framing for the narrative spine"):**

- **9.7 applies to the main narrative of the deck** — the framework visual, the organizing metaphor, the central argument. Catalog-of-options there dilutes the message (9.1). Pick one.
- **9.2 applies to format and layout decisions** where both options are defensible and her preference is useful to learn.

**Signaling to Marta when the two-options move is used:** a short cover note helps — *"Showing two layouts here because I wanted your eye on this; the framework visual is a single choice."* Prevents the offering from reading as indecisive drafting.

---

### 9.3 Strip the meta-labels

Meta-labels announce the slide's position in a taxonomy and crowd out the message. In the April 19 review she cut "Thematic area 2:", "Study 1 of 2", "Framework visual — Option 1: Pyramid", the "(option 2)" tag on the tools slide, the "— or —" between two subtitle options, and the page counters ("7 / 18").

The slide's title *is* the topic. Labels that tell the reader "this is the second thematic area" add scaffolding that serves navigation, not message — and per 9.1, message wins.

**Rule for drafters:** On slide titles, name the topic directly. Do not write "Thematic area N: X" — just write "X". Do not write "Study 1 of 2 / Study 2 of 2" — the study name is enough. Do not include page counters unless the deck has 30+ slides and navigation becomes genuinely useful.

---

### 9.4 Group studies by sub-topic, don't flat-list them

When a thematic area has four or more items, she groups them under short sub-topic headers. Example from the April 19 tax-policy slide she rewrote:

> **Tax design** — Tax expenditures; IVA-P
> **Digital transformation of tax administration** — e-billing payoff (El Salvador)
> **Tax compliance** — CONFIA cooperative compliance; autorregularização (Brazil states)
> **Tax dispute resolutions** — AI-assisted dispute resolution (Peru Tribunal Fiscal)

Grouping is how the "how" (9.1) shows up on a thematic-area slide: the sub-topics reveal the shape of the agenda. A flat bullet list of seven items reads as *what is in this* (catalog); the grouped version reads as *here is the structure we've chosen to cover* (message).

**Rule for drafters:** On thematic-area slides with 4+ items, group studies under 2–4 sub-topic headers. Use short sub-headers (one to three words). Place 1–2 items under each. If a sub-topic has no current study, leave it as a placeholder — it still communicates the shape of the agenda.

---

### 9.5 Trim per-item descriptions to research question + instrument

On an overview slide, each item should carry the research question and the instrument label — nothing more. Her edit to the e-billing item:

> **Before (v3.1):** "The e-billing payoff — El Salvador's VAT compliance response. Did El Salvador's rollout of electronic billing raise VAT compliance? Event-study estimation using Callaway and Sant'Anna's staggered difference-in-differences, exploiting the phased introduction of e-billing across taxpayer groups. Impact evaluation."
>
> **After (April 19):** "The e-billing payoff: Did El Salvador's rollout of electronic billing raise VAT compliance? Impact evaluation."

The research question carries the "why" in compressed form. The instrument label carries a lightweight "how." Method-name detail (Callaway and Sant'Anna, staggered DiD) is credential, not message — the exact case 9.1 warns against.

**Rule for drafters:** On overview and thematic-area slides, each bullet gets (a) a short name, (b) the research question, (c) the instrument label (impact evaluation, technical note, how-to note, diagnostic, platform). Method, identification strategy, and data source go elsewhere — to a deep-dive slide, a working paper, or a ToR.

---

### 9.6 Cut the "We continue developing" scaffolding

v3.1 thematic-area slides had two sections: *"We continue developing the agenda on:"* (ongoing) and *"Moving the agenda forward on:"* (new this year). She dropped the first one entirely on the slides she rewrote.

The ongoing-work line was there to reassure the reader that FMM does more than the highlighted studies. Per 9.1, that reassurance is a catalog claim ("here is the list of things we do"); the slide's real estate belongs to the message about what is being moved forward and why.

**Rule for drafters:** On thematic-area slides, lead with current-year work. If ongoing-work context is needed, put it in a single short line at the top (e.g., "Continuing platforms: FISLAC, LAPEF") — not a full section with its own header.

---

### 9.7 One clear framing for the narrative spine

The main narrative of a deck — the framework visual, the organizing metaphor, the central argument — should be one clear choice, not a menu. The April 17 deck had both a pyramid (Slide 2) and a two-mode side-by-side map (Slide 3) with a note saying "Marta to choose". By April 19 she had deleted the pyramid, modified the side-by-side, and added the cross-sector themes as a fourth panel within it.

Two options on the narrative spine is a catalog of possibilities. One option is a decision — which is a message about how you see the agenda.

The exact boundary between "narrative spine" (one choice) and "layout/format decision" (two options OK, per 9.2) is something the next few reviews will help calibrate. For now: framework visuals, organizing metaphors, subtitle choices, and the argument structure of a flagship slide are narrative spine. Table-vs-prose on a tools slide, or grouping headers on a thematic-area slide, are layout decisions.

**Rule for drafters:** Show one framework visual. If genuinely uncertain between two, present the preferred one and flag the alternative in a cover note — don't put both in the deck.

---

### 9.8 4–5 representative items per slide, not a full catalog

On the April 19 tax-policy slide she cut the list from seven items to four, and commented to add 1–2 more *representative* studies — not to restore the full list. The target is representative-of-this-year, not comprehensive.

Seven items is a catalog; four or five chosen to represent the range of sub-topics and methods is a message about the shape of the agenda.

**Rule for drafters:** Aim for 4–5 items per thematic-area slide. If the agenda has more, pick the ones that best represent the range of sub-topics and methods. Keep a full list in the skeleton document for reference, not on the slide.

---

### 9.9 Sub-topic headers read flat, not like chapter labels

"Tax design" — not "Tax design research agenda." "Procurement" — not "Procurement: open research questions." The header names the bucket in one or two words and stops.

This is the 9.4 rule refined: sub-topic headers do their work by being short enough to scan — readers should see the shape of the agenda from the headers alone. Longer headers that try to pre-summarize the bucket compete with the items below them for attention, and readers end up processing the same information twice.

**Rule for drafters:** Sub-topic headers on thematic-area slides are noun phrases, not sentences. One to three words. No colons, no qualifiers.

---

### 9.10 Table format when the content is tabular

On the April 19 tools slide she chose the table format (Tool / What it does / Emphasis 2026) over a grouped-prose alternative that was also offered. That is one preference expressed once — not enough to generalize firmly.

**Working hypothesis:** when every item has the same small number of attributes (3–5) and those attributes are short, a table reads more cleanly than parallel prose paragraphs.

Tables align with 9.1 when done well: "what each tool does" and "emphasis this year" are compressed how-and-why columns. A prose paragraph about FISLAC that wraps those facts in narrative language smuggles in catalog content that the table cleanly leaves out.

**How to generate more evidence:** next time a tools / platforms / instruments slide is drafted, propose to Andrea offering Marta two layouts per 9.2 — table vs. grouped prose — and see which she picks. Repeat on one or two more cases; if she keeps picking the table, this rule firms up. If the preference is context-dependent, we'll see the contexts.

**Rule for drafters (for now):** Default to a table when every item has the same small number of short attributes. Default to prose when descriptions are narrative or of uneven length. When both are defensible, propose the two-option offer to Andrea.

---

### 9.11 Don't over-apply these rules outside of slide decks

Two rules in Section 8 can be misread as conflicting with Section 9:

- **Section 8.2 says "Remove bold lead-ins on paragraphs."** That rule applies to *prose drafts* (notes, briefs, memos). It is about inline bold inside continuous prose, not about section structure. Slide decks legitimately use distinct sub-topic headers (9.4) — that is structural organization, not inline emphasis.
- **Section 8.4 says "Match section headers to actual content."** In a deck, the corollary is that the slide's title is the organizing statement. Don't layer taxonomic labels on top of a title that already names the topic (9.3).

Treat Sections 8 and 9 as complementary: Section 8 governs the prose on the slide; Section 9 governs how the slide is organized.

**Note on relationship between Sections 8 and 9:** the core logic is shared — strip what doesn't carry the message, keep what does. Once more review cycles accumulate, Sections 8 and 9 may be refactored around a single cross-format principle. For now, they stand as two expressions of the same underlying approach applied to different media.

---

### 9.12 Summary: The Marta slide pass

After finishing a draft deck, run this pass:

1. **Apply 9.0 first.** Confirm the conceptual framework can be stated in one sentence and the narrative arc in 3–5 sentences. Confirm there is an explicit framework slide early in the deck. If either fails, fix the deck-level architecture before doing anything else.
2. **Apply 9.1.** On every substantive slide, confirm the why (gap / moment) and the how (intellectual move) are visible. If either is vague, the slide will drift — fix before polishing.
3. Cut every meta-label ("Thematic area N:", "Study N of N", "Option N:", page counters).
4. On thematic-area slides, check the item count: 4–5 representative items, not a full catalog.
5. If any thematic-area slide has 4+ items and they cluster, add short sub-topic headers (1–3 words each) and regroup.
6. Trim each item to short name + research question + instrument label. Cut method detail.
7. Drop "We continue developing the agenda on:" sections; lead with current-year work.
8. If two framework visuals are in the deck, delete one.
9. Tabular content that fits a 3–5 slot pattern → table. Narrative or uneven content → prose. When both are defensible, propose offering Marta the choice (9.2).
10. Run the Section 8 prose pass on the text remaining on each slide.
11. If the deck is a portfolio rather than a story (the 9.0 arc genuinely doesn't fit), give it explicit numbered section dividers, one before each block (9.13). If it tells one story, don't add them.

---

### 9.13 When a deck can't tell one story, make the structure explicit — numbering is the fallback, not the default

9.0 requires every deck to have a narrative arc and a named conceptual framework. But some decks are genuinely *portfolios*, not stories — the 2026 Knowledge Agenda is four parallel lines of work (strategic positioning / thematic research / cross-sector analysis / operational instruments), not one sequential narrative. For that kind of deck, the question 9.0 leaves open is: how do you give structure to material that has no single thread?

Marta's answer, shipped in the Knowledge Agenda final: when the arc exists, let it carry the audience and add nothing; when it doesn't, make the structure **explicit and navigable** — and numbering the blocks, with a short divider slide re-asserting the numbered spine before each block, is the legitimate tool. The final deck has four numbered section dividers ("1 — Strategic positioning research," "2 — Thematic research," etc.), one before each block.

The preference order matters:

1. **If the deck can tell one story**, the arc carries it. Do not add numbered scaffolding — that is the meta-label clutter 9.3 warns against, and an arc that works needs no signposts.
2. **If the material is genuinely parallel and will not reduce to one arc**, make the structure explicit. Numbered blocks with re-asserted dividers are the honest way to give a portfolio navigability it can't get from a narrative it doesn't have.

**How this reconciles with 9.3.** 9.3 strips *redundant per-slide labels* ("Thematic area N:", page counters) because they serve navigation the slide's own title already provides. The section dividers here are different: they are *deliberate structural breaks in a long, multi-block, non-narrative deck*, carrying navigation the individual slide titles cannot. The line is: strip labels that duplicate what's already on the slide; keep numbered dividers that give a portfolio the spine it otherwise lacks. Numbering is never free — it's a cost you pay only when an arc isn't available.

**Rule for drafters:** Try for the arc first (9.0). If the deck genuinely cannot be one story, state that, then give it explicit numbered structure with a divider before each block. Don't number a deck that already tells a story; don't leave a portfolio deck without explicit structure.

## 10. Summary: The Marta Edit in One Pass (prose drafts)

After completing a prose draft in Marta's voice, run this final edit pass:

1. **Cut the sales pitch.** Delete any sentence whose only function is to tell the reader the work is impressive.
2. **Break up dense lists.** If 3+ parallel examples sit in one paragraph, convert to bullets with a framing intro.
3. **Remove bold paragraph openers.** Structure comes from headings, not inline bold.
4. **Kill redundancy.** If a point is made in the closing, don't preview it in the opening. If a partnership/initiative is mentioned in one section, don't give it its own paragraph in another.
5. **Trim low-power detail.** Sub-details that are accurate but don't strengthen the case get cut.
6. **Cut uncommitted plans.** Only include work that is concrete and underway, not aspirational.
7. **Check scope.** Does the opening accurately reflect the breadth of what's covered?
8. **Match headers to content.** After cuts, do the section titles still fit?
9. **Clean up punctuation.** Replace em-dashes with periods or "so" where appropriate.
10. **Name publications simply.** "Forthcoming study," not "analytical volume," until it's published.

For slide decks, run the Section 9 pass instead of (or in addition to) this one.

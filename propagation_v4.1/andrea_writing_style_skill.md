# Andrea Lopez-Luzuriaga — Academic Writing Style Profile

**Purpose:** This document describes the writing voice, habits, and stylistic patterns of Andrea Lopez-Luzuriaga, based on close reading of her job market paper *"Less is More? Limits to Itemized Deductions and Tax Evasion."* Use this profile to write in her voice when producing academic papers, policy notes, and analytical documents. This profile is self-contained: it provides all the guidance needed to write in Andrea's academic voice without reference to any other document.

---

## How to Use This Profile

**Work from an outline.** Before drafting any document, produce a detailed outline of the whole document and get Andrea's approval. Then work through each section: outline the section in detail, get comments, and only then draft the prose. This ensures the argument is structured and complete before any writing begins. Do not skip ahead to drafting a later section without approval on the current one.

**Draft first, style second.** When producing a document, draft the content first and get Andrea's approval on substance, structure, and argument before applying this style profile. Do not attempt to produce a polished final product in one pass. Andrea works iteratively — she provides information in stages and expects to review and approve before the agent moves forward.

**Do not infer — ask.** If information is missing or unclear, do not fill in the gaps by guessing. For instance, if Andrea adds a point without explaining why it matters, do not infer the motivation — ask her. The agent may suggest a possible reason ("Could the motivation be X?"), but should always get confirmation before writing. This applies especially to:
- Descriptions of models, diagrams, figures, or conceptual frameworks, where the underlying logic needs to come from Andrea
- Background or institutional context that the agent cannot verify
- The overall objective of a document (see Abstract Style section)
- Any case where the agent is unsure whether a claim should be hedged or stated directly

**Clarifying questions are always welcome.** The agent should never hesitate to ask Andrea for clarification. It is always better to ask a question and get it right than to draft something based on assumptions. Ask before rewriting, not after.

**Before starting any document, confirm the following with Andrea:**
- What type of document is this? (academic paper, policy note, working paper, etc.)
- Who is the audience? (other economists in the field, a general academic audience, policymakers, etc.)
- What is the overall objective or core argument?
- Is this co-authored? If so, with whom? (This determines whether to use "I" or "we")
- What language is the final product in? (English, Spanish, Portuguese)
- Are there journal or submission formatting requirements?

**Before applying the style profile to a draft, verify with Andrea:**
- Is the purpose of the document clear? Can you state it in one sentence?
- Is the core argument present and complete?
- Does every citation serve a specific claim?
- Does every data point have a reason for being included?
- Does every figure, model, or diagram description have a clear role in the argument?
- Is the audience defined?
- Are there sections that feel incomplete or where the motivation is unclear?

If any of these are missing or unclear, flag them for Andrea before rewriting. The style profile should be applied to a draft whose substance is solid — not used to polish a draft that is still missing its core argument.

## 1. Overall Voice and Stance

Andrea writes as an **economist who thinks like a storyteller.** She leads with intuition before formality: the reader always understands *why something matters* before seeing the model or the regression. Her tone is **confident but not aggressive** — she presents her contribution clearly and lets the evidence do the persuading. She does not oversell results; instead, she is careful to scope her claims (e.g., "this result needs to be understood in the institutional context of Ecuador").

She positions herself as someone offering a **new lens on a known problem**, not someone overturning a literature. Her framing is typically: "The literature has established X. I show that there is also Y, which has been overlooked." She respects prior work while carving her own space.

The aspiration is writing where the reader feels clever for following the argument — not writing that forces the reader to decode it. The point should feel obvious and well-constructed, even when the reasoning is sophisticated.

---

## 2. Sentence Structure and Length

- **Sentences are long and information-dense**, often 30–50 words. She packs multiple ideas into a single sentence, connected by semicolons, commas, and subordinate clauses. Short punchy sentences are rare.
- She favors **complex sentences with qualifying phrases** embedded mid-sentence:
  > "Taxpayers can interpret the *itemizing* threshold as equivalent to an *auditing* threshold, and infer that claiming a larger deduction does not meaningfully increase their probability of being audited, as long as it is less than the itemizing threshold."
- She uses **semicolons liberally** to connect closely related independent clauses rather than starting new sentences.
- Parenthetical clarifications appear frequently, both in parentheses and as mid-sentence asides set off by commas.

**Aspirational note:** When writing for Andrea, aim to occasionally break the longest sentences (60+ words) into two. The guiding principle is that the reader should never have to hold too much information in working memory to reach the payoff. The argument should feel easy to follow — each sentence should land clearly enough that the reader feels the point is obvious, even when the reasoning is sophisticated. Preserve long, clause-heavy sentences in methodology and data sections where precision requires packing conditions and qualifications together. Break them in introductions, conclusions, and anywhere the core argument needs to land fast. The aspiration is writing where the reader feels clever for following it — not writing that makes the reader work to decode it.

---

## 3. Paragraph Structure

- **Paragraphs are long** — often 8–15 sentences. She does not break ideas into many small paragraphs. A single paragraph may contain an argument, its evidence, a qualification, and a transition.
- **Topic sentences are clear and direct.** She typically opens with a claim or a setup, then develops it within the paragraph. She rarely uses rhetorical questions or dramatic openings.
- **Every paragraph must land.** The paragraph should not orbit the topic — it should move forward and deliver a conclusion. A common failure mode, especially in literature reviews and background sections, is the circular paragraph: one that restates context, adds qualifiers, references the literature, circles back, and ends where it started without ever delivering a claim. If a paragraph has no conclusion or "so what," it probably doesn't need to exist.
- **Test:** After drafting any paragraph, ask: "If the reader only reads the first and last sentence, do they get the point?" If not, restructure.
- Transitions between paragraphs are **functional rather than flashy**: "I complement this identification strategy with...", "To provide further evidence...", "One alternative explanation for..."
- She often closes a section or paragraph with a **policy-relevant takeaway** or a forward-looking sentence that sets up the next section.

**Aspirational note:** Strengthen transitions by occasionally adding *motivation* before describing the next analytical step. For example, instead of jumping to "I complement this with a difference-in-difference estimation," lead with the concern that motivates it: "A natural concern is that taxpayers amend returns for reasons unrelated to the reform. To address this, I complement..." Andrea does this sometimes but not consistently — doing it more would improve narrative flow.

## 4. How She Introduces Arguments

- She uses a **"the literature established X, and I build on it to show Y"** structure. The tone is constructive, not combative — she positions her contribution as extending and complementing existing work, not correcting it. The framing is typically: "The literature has established X. I show that there is also Y, which has been overlooked." She sounds well-informed and lets the quality of the work speak for itself rather than overclaiming the contribution. **Unless specifically instructed otherwise, default to this constructive framing.** Do not use language that implies the literature was wrong or that prior work missed something obvious — frame the gap as something that had not yet been explored, not something others failed to see.
- The introduction follows a clear arc: (1) general framing of the problem, (2) what the literature thinks, (3) what she argues is missing, (4) preview of her mechanism, (5) institutional setting, (6) identification strategy, (7) key results, (8) broader implications.
- She uses **"In brief,"** and **"In practical terms,"** to pivot from technical discussion to plain-language explanation. This is a distinctive habit.
- She occasionally uses vivid analogies in otherwise dry academic prose — notably the "Schrödinger's audit" metaphor, which is creative and memorable.
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** Do not spend paragraphs describing what the paper will do before doing it. Roadmap sentences in an introduction should be at most one or two sentences. If the structure is clear from section headings and topic sentences, the roadmap may not be needed at all. Begin making the argument from the first paragraph.

**Aspirational note:** Lean into the analogies more. One well-placed analogy per paper, especially when introducing the core mechanism, can make the difference between a paper people cite and a paper people *remember*. Andrea has the instinct for this; the goal is to give herself permission to use it.

## 5. Technical vs. Intuitive Explanation

This is one of her most distinctive qualities: **she always gives the intuition first, then the formalism.** This applies to models, equations, graphs, figures, and conceptual frameworks alike.

- Before the model section, she explains the logic in the introduction in plain language ("In brief, taxpayers know that some amount of claimed deduction would be high enough to draw scrutiny, but not have any clear sense of what that amount might be").
- When presenting the model, she uses phrases like "For ease of exposition," "It is easiest to analyze this scenario in the space for income and deductions," and "In the most extreme case."
- After equations, she immediately explains what each term means in real-world language.
- She uses the phrase **"In practical terms"** to translate formal results into behavioral predictions.
- She describes taxpayer behavior as rational decision-making, always grounding the math in what a person would actually think or do.

### Describing Models, Graphs, and Concepts

When describing models, graphical representations, or conceptual frameworks, Andrea **narrates them as a series of decisions that build onto the argument.** The description is not a static label of what exists in the figure — it is a guided walk through the logic, where each element is motivated by what the agent faces and how they respond.

- Rather than simply labeling regions or cases, she walks the reader through each one by describing the agent's situation, incentives, and rational response. The reader understands the graph as a map of behavior, not just a partition of parameter space.
- For example, instead of "Region B is defined by $y < h^A$," she writes: "A taxpayer in Region B believes that her income is lower than the auditing threshold. If she chooses a deduction lower than or equal to the auditing threshold, the tax authority will not verify her deductions... Her incentive is to take a deduction equal to her income, moving to the zero-tax line."
- Each region, case, or stage in a model is not just described but **motivated** — the reader understands *why* an agent ends up there and *what they would do*, so the diagram advances the argument rather than merely illustrating it.
- The same principle applies to conceptual frameworks: build the framework incrementally, stage by stage, so the reader understands how each piece connects to the next. In her JMP, the conceptual framework is organized by stages of information (pre-reform beliefs → post-reform updating → combined model with hassle cost), and each stage is explained in behavioral terms before introducing the formalism.

**The guiding principle is: describe what you show in a way that builds onto the argument.** A figure description should leave the reader understanding the mechanism better, not just knowing what the axes are.

## 6. Hedging and Confidence

- Andrea hedges carefully but not excessively. She uses:
  - "can" and "could" rather than "will" when discussing potential behaviors: "This could have similar effects on compliance."
  - "I show evidence that..." rather than "I prove that..."
  - "potentially leading to..." for uncertain aggregate effects.
- She is **more direct when stating her core findings**: "I show that an itemizing threshold conveys information," "I observe that these taxpayers increased their deductions," "I conclude that the introduction of the itemizing threshold effectively decreases the uncertainty."
- She scopes her claims geographically and institutionally: "This result needs to be understood in the institutional context of Ecuador and, more generally, in developing countries."

---

## 7. Use of Evidence and Data

- She provides **concrete numbers** frequently and precisely: "$1,543 average increase," "3.6% of total employees," "23 cents of each dollar of taxable income." She does not round loosely.
- She gives **scale context**: "to give a sense of scale, the average original deduction was $882, and the average reported tax liability was $712."
- She cross-references model predictions against data patterns explicitly: *"Prediction: Not all taxpayers who increase their deductions take a deduction at the itemizing threshold."* Then she tests the prediction.
- She often includes **institutional detail** from conversations with practitioners: "according to conversations with tax authority employees," "at least so the Ecuadorian tax authority claims in the refund request form."

---

## 8. Literature Integration

- Citations are clustered, often listing 3–6 papers in a single parenthetical reference.
- When reviewing the literature, she **describes each paper's contribution in 1–3 sentences**, with specifics about the country, mechanism, and finding.
- She links papers to her argument by showing how each fits a pattern, then explains what her paper adds: "In summary, deductions are a significant margin for tax non-compliance... In this paper, I show that..."

**Aspirational note:** In standalone literature review sections, organize around *mechanisms* rather than sequentially by paper. Group papers by what they show (hassle cost effects, information effects, enforcement effects) rather than walking through each one in turn. Andrea's closing paragraphs in lit review sections already do this well ("In summary, deductions are a significant margin..."); the body of the review could match that structure. However, when integrating citations within the introduction or empirical sections, the current approach — one to three sentences per paper, clustered in parenthetical references — works well and should be preserved. The distinction is: literature review sections argue about a body of evidence; other sections cite specific papers to support specific claims.

## 9. Recurring Phrases and Verbal Habits

These phrases appear often and are characteristic of her voice:

- **"In particular,"** — used to narrow from general to specific
- **"In practical terms,"** — used to translate technical findings into behavioral language
- **"In brief,"** — used to preview or summarize
- **"I take advantage of..."** — used to introduce identification strategies
- **"Notice that..."** — used to draw attention to a feature of a model or result
- **"Therefore,"** — used frequently for logical consequence
- **"As a result,"** — similar function
- **"Hence,"** — used less often but present
- **"To the best of my knowledge"** — used for claims she cannot fully verify
- **"I complement this... with..."** — used when adding a robustness check or second strategy
- **"There could be a concern that..."** — used to introduce and then address potential objections
- She uses **"she" and "her"** as the default pronoun for the generic taxpayer.

**Phrases to avoid** (these sound wrong in Andrea's academic voice):

- **"Crucially,"** / **"Importantly,"** — too dramatic; Andrea signals importance through argument structure, not adverbs
- **"It is well-established that..."** — sounds like throat-clearing; if it's well-established, cite and move on
- **"This paper contributes to the literature by..."** — too self-promotional; prefer showing the contribution through the "literature established X, I show Y" structure
- **"The results are striking / surprising / remarkable"** — Andrea lets results speak for themselves; she does not editorialize about how impressive they are
- **"We show that..." / "We find that..."** — Andrea uses "I" consistently in single-authored work
- **"Needless to say,"** — if it is needless to say, don't say it
- **"It is worth noting that..."** — if it's worth noting, just note it
- **"It is important to highlight that..."** — same problem; just state the point

## 10. Section and Paper Organization

- She follows a conventional empirical economics structure: Introduction → Literature Review → Conceptual Framework → Background → Data and Empirical Strategy → Results → Conclusions.
- The introduction is **very detailed** — essentially a self-contained summary of the paper. It previews the mechanism, the institutional setting, the identification strategy, and the key numbers.
- The conceptual framework section is organized by *stages of information*: pre-reform beliefs → post-reform updating → combined model with hassle cost. This reflects her thinking: she builds understanding incrementally.
- Conclusions include both academic contribution and **practical policy recommendations**, grounded in institutional knowledge.

**Aspirational note:** Reduce redundancy between the introduction and later sections. When the empirical strategy or institutional background has already been described in detail in the introduction, later sections can reference back rather than re-explain. This tightens the paper and respects the reader's attention.

---

## 11. What She Does NOT Do

- She does **not** use bullet points or numbered lists in running text.
- She does **not** use casual or conversational register in academic writing.
- She does **not** use first-person plural ("we") — she uses **"I"** consistently in single-authored work. **Always ask Andrea whether a document is co-authored before drafting.** When co-authoring, "we" replaces "I" throughout; all other stylistic conventions remain the same.
- She does **not** overuse hedging words; when she has a finding, she states it directly.
- She **defaults to active voice with "I" as the subject.** Passive voice occasionally appears in data description passages (e.g., "Analysis of the characteristics... allows for the observation that...") and should generally be corrected to active constructions like "I observe that..." However, passive voice is acceptable when it is genuinely the most effective way to express something — for instance, when the agent of the action is irrelevant or when the object needs emphasis. The goal is active voice as the default, not as an absolute rule.
- She does **not** write circular paragraphs that restate context without delivering a conclusion. Paragraphs move forward.
- She does **not** editorialize about how important or striking a finding is — she presents the finding and lets it land.
- She does **not** spend paragraphs describing what the paper will do before doing it. Roadmaps are brief.

## 12. Abstract Style

The abstract should give a reader enough information to know whether the paper is useful to read for what they need. A reader working on the same mechanism, the same policy instrument, or the same empirical context should be able to identify the paper as relevant from the abstract alone.

Before drafting an abstract, check the project instructions or ask Andrea for the overall objective of the document. The abstract should be calibrated to that objective. If the paper uses a specific country or reform to illuminate a general mechanism, the abstract should lead with the mechanism and mention the empirical setting as the vehicle — not the other way around. If the paper is fundamentally about a country-specific policy question, the setting belongs up front.

The structure is: (1) one to two sentences framing the question and the mechanism, (2) enough detail on the empirical setting and strategy for credibility, without making the setting the protagonist if it isn't, (3) one to two sentences on key results — include numbers when they strengthen the case but do not overload with detail, (4) one sentence on the policy implication or broader significance.

Prioritize the core contribution and the most compelling result. If the paper has multiple empirical strategies, mention the primary one briefly; do not enumerate all of them. Keep abstracts under 150 words unless the journal or submission requires otherwise.

## 13. Tense Consistency

Maintain consistent tense usage across the document. Establish the tense logic at the start of a draft and maintain it throughout. The general conventions are:

- **Present tense** for what the paper does, argues, or shows: "I show that...," "This paper explores...," "The model predicts that..."
- **Past tense** for what happened in the empirical setting or what agents did: "Taxpayers increased their deductions...," "The tax authority introduced the itemizing requirement...," "Ecuador implemented the reform in 2009..."
- **Past tense** for what other authors found: "Kleven et al. (2011) showed that...," "Prior work found evidence that..."
- **Present tense** for general truths, mechanisms, and standing institutional features: "Deductions reduce taxable income...," "Taxpayers face a trade-off between...," "The Ecuadorian tax code allows..."

If the appropriate tense for a particular passage is unclear — for instance, when describing an institutional feature that may or may not still be in effect — ask Andrea before drafting rather than guessing.

## 14. Language and Translation

Andrea drafts in English and often translates the final product into Spanish or Portuguese. The structure, conciseness, and forward-moving paragraph logic of the English version should be preserved in translation. Spanish and Portuguese prose conventions tend toward longer setups, more qualifiers, more circular paragraph structures, and more throat-clearing — exactly the patterns this profile pushes against. When translating or drafting in Spanish or Portuguese, do not allow the text to drift back toward those conventions. The translated version should read as clearly and directly as the English original.

When the deliverable is translated into Spanish, the translation runs through `translation_review_skill.md` — the naturalness review and correction-capture pass. It works *below* this section's discipline (sentence-level naturalness: punctuation habits, connectors, calques, register), never against it: the structure and conciseness rules above hold in the Spanish.
